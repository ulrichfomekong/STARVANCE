Iowa Crop Knowledge and Rotation Dataset: Research Report (revision 4, draft v0.1)
Scope and evidence rules
This report covers the agronomic knowledge component for the Iowa crop rotation decision-support tool. It excludes NASA environmental datasets. Every claim here traces to a source ID in source_register.csv, and each source carries a verification status. A source is verified when its page or document was opened and the claim was checked against it. Secondary reprints are marked as such and are not counted as independent sources. Only claims from verified sources are used. Claims that depend only on blocked sources have been removed. Research results come from specific sites, years and management systems. They are evidence about those studies, not proof that these exact rotations will produce the same results on any Iowa field.
Main findings
Corn after soybean outperforms corn after corn. At the Nashua Northeast Research Farm (single location, 1979 to 2004 averages), corn after soybean averaged about 16 bu/ac more than corn after corn, with a lower N response (S01, S36). The authors caution that N responses come from one location.
Soybean gives a modest N credit, much smaller than alfalfa's and not alfalfa-like. Estimates differ by period and method. A recent peer-reviewed synthesis of long-term Iowa and Illinois sites reports the corn-after-soybean EONR advantage shrinking from about 44 kg N/ha (about 39 lb/ac) in 2000 to 32 kg N/ha (about 29 lb/ac) in 2020 (S35). Older Iowa extension work estimated an apparent soybean contribution of 52 to 60 lb N/ac (S04). The dataset keeps a range of about 29 to 60 lb N/ac as a literature comparison, not as an operational fertilizer credit. The MVP does not subtract any amount of nitrogen from a recommended application rate, and the range makes no alfalfa-like assumption. An apparent nitrogen contribution, an EONR difference and a fertilizer credit are different measurements and are not interchangeable. Soybean yield itself was not affected by N applied to the previous corn (S03 is blocked; the same finding appears in S36).
Alfalfa gives the largest and most consistent corn benefit, and the N credit is conditional. First-year corn after alfalfa averaged 8 to 18 percent higher yield than continuous corn and 0 to 8 percent higher than corn after soybean in Wisconsin and northern Iowa long-term trials (S24). Second-year corn averaged 0 to 8 percent above continuous corn but 2 to 8 percent below corn after soybean (S24). In the Minnesota 259-trial review, first-year corn on medium-textured soil after 3-year-plus alfalfa responded to fertilizer N in 5 percent of trials when fall-terminated and 17 percent when spring-terminated (S27). Response probability also depends on seeding method: 2-year direct-seeded stands responded in 8 percent of trials, and 2-year companion-seeded stands in 35 percent (S27). About half of second-year corn fields in Iowa and Minnesota showed no yield response to added N (S23, S10). Soil tests predicted response with only 57 to 60 percent accuracy (S10).
Alfalfa nitrogen figures are not interchangeable. The 75 lb N/ac figure (Minnesota) and the 100 lb N/ac figure (reported for Iowa) differ in jurisdiction, concept and status. Full detail is in alfalfa_nitrogen_guidance.csv. Summary:
• The Minnesota figures (S10, S27) are book-value fertilizer credits for second-year corn after a good stand (0/50/75 lb N/ac by stand class). They are rate reductions, validated only partly (31 percent accuracy in second-year trials, S10).
• The 100 lb N/ac Iowa figure appears only in a secondary article (S23). It has not been confirmed against current Iowa State guidance and must not be used.
• Current Iowa State guidance (S39) sets rates for each rotation (corn after soybean and corn after corn) without subtracting a legume credit. The sources reviewed do not publish a corn-after-alfalfa rate for Iowa.
• The Iowa Nashua data (S36) show a measured response pattern: first-year corn after alfalfa responds little to N, and second-year corn falls between corn after soybean and corn after corn. The 2006 presentation (S40) describes second-year corn after alfalfa as resembling corn after soybean, which conflicts with S36. The paper's rotation codes are ambiguous.
• Wisconsin guidance (S21) says termination timing does not change the credit; Minnesota evidence (S09, S27) says fall termination lowers the first-year guideline slightly. The dataset records this as an open disagreement.
• Alfalfa stand N content (up to 200 lb N/ac in S27; up to 300 lb in S23) is estimated N content, not a fertilizer credit, and is not used as a credit.
Establishment matters for the N response. In the Minnesota trials, second-year corn after direct-seeded alfalfa showed a lower frequency of response to additional fertilizer N than corn after companion-seeded alfalfa (S27). Scenario A uses direct-seeded alfalfa as a project assumption. The MVP Scenario A uses direct-seeded alfalfa with no oat nurse crop (scenario_decisions.md).
Oats are a grain phase, not a nitrogen-fixer. Earlier seeding raised oat yield and test weight; yield plateaued near 20 plants per square foot; underseeding alfalfa did not change oat yield or test weight in organic trials (S19). In S07, oats responded to fertilizer N except after two years of alfalfa (S07). Spring small grains generally mature before the end of July in Iowa (S33, a 2008 repost). Oats are described as a financial weak link in rotations (S19). Conventional oat yield, straw and economics were not established, because the Iowa straw source (S20) is blocked.
Cereal rye effects on corn are real but inconsistent. In a five-site ISU study (2009 to 2013), the economic optimum N rate was similar with and without rye (157 vs 151 lb N/ac), yield was 5 percent lower with rye (179 vs 188 bu/ac), and rye carried only 8 to 26 lb N/ac (S12). A later study found a 2 percent difference (S12). On-farm strips across Iowa found little or no yield loss in corn-soybean rotations (S17). Termination 10 to 14 days before corn planting is the recommendation in the verified Iowa State source (S13). Iowa State extension considers allelopathy probably minor compared with mulch effects, with possible N tie-up and a pathogen green-bridge effect (S13). Terminating rye at soybean planting, compared with no cover, reduced waterhemp seed production by more than 65 percent in one Boone trial, but a residual herbicide was still needed (S15). Farmers report crop-insurance termination deadlines of on or before soybean planting in the western third of Iowa and before soybean emergence in the eastern two-thirds (S34). The RMA policy text has not been opened.
Red clover (Iowa values are dated). ISU's 2006 publication gives a pH optimum above 6.0, tolerance of poorly drained soils but not saturated ones, a frost-seeding rate of 15 to 20 lb/ac, and an average fertilizer N replacement value of about 80 lb/ac for corn (S37). Wisconsin guidance gives a first-year credit equal to 80 percent of alfalfa's for similar stands (S22). No current Iowa credit table was found.
Cover-crop and interseeding evidence is thin. Interseeded oats and alfalfa in corn showed no yield difference in one 2024 on-farm trial (S18); the cooperator judged that trial unfair because of weed pressure. Treat it as a single-site result.
Longer rotations show erosion and disease benefits, with a caveat. In the ten-year central Iowa study (Liebman; secondary reprint S08), soil loss fell from 1.36 to 0.88 ton/ac/yr as rotations lengthened, sudden death syndrome was much higher in the two-year rotation, and corn yields were 188, 194 and 197 bu/ac. The four-year corn-soybean-oats/alfalfa-alfalfa rotation in that study is a four-year rotation resembling the project's optional Scenario C, not Scenario A. Scenario C is not part of the implemented MVP. Economic results from that study are not used.
Disagreements and open questions
• Soybean N credit: 29 to 39 lb N/ac (S35) vs 50 to 60 lb N/ac (S04). The range is kept, not resolved.
• Second-year corn after alfalfa: 0 to 8 percent above continuous corn (S24) vs 6 to 8 percent (S09, S25, the same article). S09 and S25 count as one evidence source.
• Second-year corn after alfalfa response vs corn after soybean: similar (S40) vs intermediate (S36). The Iowa dataset (S36) is preferred because it has explicit tables.
• Fall vs spring alfalfa termination: no effect on credit (S21, S22) vs slightly lower first-year guideline for fall (S09, S27).
• Morris et al. (1993) Iowa sites: 3 of 29 positive net return (S04 text) vs 6 responsive sites (S04 Table 2).
Limitations
Most Iowa data come from research farms and on-farm trials. Some are organic. Four Iowa State Digital Press sources (S02, S03, S14, S20) could not be checked: S02 was confirmed blocked, and S03, S14 and S20 were not individually tested. Their claims are not used. Stating that they are inaccessible would overstate what was tested. Several sources are secondary reprints (S08, S09/S25, S23, S33). Dated Iowa values need checking against current ISU publications (S37 is from 2006; S39 uses 2007 data). Minnesota and Wisconsin figures are not Iowa values and are labeled as such in the dataset.
Data gaps
• Soil texture and drainage for corn, soybean and oats; water requirements for soybean, oats and alfalfa.
• Corn and soybean harvest windows by calendar date (soybean harvest guidance is moisture-based, S38).
• Alfalfa planting and first-harvest windows for Iowa.
• Herbicide carryover and plant-back limits by product.
• Nutrient removal per harvest unit.
• Current Iowa corn-after-alfalfa and red clover N guidance.
• Iowa State Digital Press sources: S02 is confirmed blocked (tested directly); S03, S14 and S20 are not individually tested and access is expected to be restricted. None of their claims are used.
• RMA cover-crop termination policy text (S34 describes it).
Integration with NASA data
The current pipeline uses NASA POWER daily precipitation and temperature data alongside USDA SSURGO soil information. Other NASA and satellite-derived datasets, including SMAP soil moisture, Sentinel-2 NDVI and land-cover observations, are potential future extensions. They are not implemented inputs in the current MVP. Their possible uses are described below and should be read as design intent, not as current capability. Root-zone soil moisture and precipitation can inform the cereal rye termination window and planting timing, which are sensitive to wet springs (S17, S13). Satellite vegetation indices such as NDVI can provide supplementary observations of vegetation greenness and canopy development, including alfalfa regrowth and rye green-up. They cannot independently confirm stand quality, plant density or termination readiness, so they must be read alongside field inspection and agronomic information. They are not yield or soil-fertility measurements. Temperature records may support future planting-window and frost-risk rules (S28, S30), but these rules are not currently implemented in the MVP. Land-cover data can confirm the preceding crop, which selects the correct rule. SSURGO soil suitability indicators (drainage class, available water storage, texture) are kept separate from nitrogen-response evidence. In the current rules, drainage triggers only the alfalfa establishment concern (R14). SSURGO drainage and texture do not determine an alfalfa nitrogen credit, and the rules do not use them for one. Pipeline and how the knowledge base connects to data
The agronomic knowledge in this report is the reference layer. It is connected to real observations as follows:
• SSURGO soil records for Story County (map units, components and horizons) are collected by collect_ssurgo_story.py. Map-unit and component values describe mapped soil at survey scale, not field measurements.
• NASA POWER daily precipitation and temperature for a grid point near Ames are collected by collect_nasa_power.py. The values are gridded estimates, not field or station measurements.
• field_profile.py reads the cleaned SSURGO files for a chosen map unit and prints its surface layer and drainage. Missing values are reported as "not recorded."
• rotation_compare.py prints the 12-year sequences for the three scenarios and attaches only the rule notes that are verified in decision_rules.json.
Detailed methods for these steps belong in a separate data-methods document, not in this report.
Support for the implemented scenarios
• Baseline (corn-soybean): The evidence supports a lower nitrogen response for corn after soybean (S01, S04, S36). The literature contains estimates of the soybean-related nitrogen advantage, but the MVP does not apply these estimates as an operational fertilizer credit or calculate a specific nitrogen reduction.
• Scenario A (corn-soybean-alfalfa-alfalfa, direct-seeded): after the second alfalfa year, a termination plan is needed before returning to corn (S27, S30). The first corn after alfalfa has a potentially lower nitrogen response, expressed qualitatively (S24, S27). Poor or very poor drainage triggers an establishment concern, not an unsuitability judgement (S30). The concern is a prompt to inspect the mapped soil conditions, not a prediction of stand failure. Direct seeding is a scenario assumption for this project, not a universal recommendation.
• Scenario B (corn-oats-soybean, oats as grain): no verified rule attaches to the oats phase yet, because the oat seeding-timing input is not defined. The demo therefore shows the sequence without a rule note for oats.
All scenarios are compared over the same 12-year horizon. No yield, profit or numeric nitrogen credit is predicted.
